Four AI concepts, explained.

Agent, skill, connector, subagent. Pick one to see what it does, with a simple example.

Choose an AI concept

An agent works toward a goal.

It can choose steps, use available tools, look at the results, and decide what to do next. A fixed automation follows preset steps; an agent can adapt its approach. Products use the term differently. Agent definition ↗

“Find two possible itineraries, compare them with our budget, and bring me a recommendation.”

In an app with suitable tools, it could search for activities, compare travel times, and revise the schedule. You review the result before booking.

Remember: the agent is the one doing the task.

A skill is a reusable playbook.

It bundles instructions and sometimes resources or code for a particular kind of task. The assistant can load a relevant skill when needed. This is a way to reuse a method, rather than retrain the model. Skills explained ↗

A “family itinerary” skill could say: include nap breaks, group nearby stops, and finish with a packing list.

A single request can say those things too. A skill becomes useful when you want to repeat the same method. It does not, by itself, grant access to your calendar.

Remember: the skill tells it how to do a type of task.

A connector links an app you use.

With your authorization, it lets the assistant retrieve information or perform supported actions in another service. The connector’s capabilities and your permissions determine what it can do. Connector guide ↗

A calendar connection could provide your available dates. A file connection could retrieve your saved travel notes.

That saves copying details between apps. Connecting a calendar is different from teaching the AI how your family likes to travel.

Remember: the connector provides access.

A subagent takes part of the work.

A lead agent can delegate a smaller task to another AI worker and combine its findings with other work. Some systems let these helpers work at the same time. Extra agents also add coordination and usage costs. How delegation works ↗

One helper researches museums, another outdoor activities; the lead agent combines their findings into a plan.

This depends on the product. Typing “use three subagents” into an ordinary chat does not guarantee that separate agents run.

Remember: a subagent handles a piece of the task.

This is an illustrative example, not a running agent or a promise that every app supports this workflow.

Do I need all four to use AI?

No. A regular conversation is enough for drafting a message or explaining an idea. Add tools when the task needs them. Agents can take longer and consume more usage; a team of agents is most useful when the work splits into meaningful independent parts. When to use agents · Delegation tradeoffs

How would I actually try a skill?

In Claude, look under Customize → Skills to browse available skills. Try a trusted one relevant to your task, such as creating a document. Simple custom skills can use written instructions; advanced ones can contain code. Read what you install and use sources you trust. Current skill guide · Skill security

And what is MCP?

Model Context Protocol is a standard that helps AI tools connect to other tools and data. Think of it as a shared way for the connection to work. You can use ready-made connectors without learning how to build one. Skills and MCP

Your apps can join the conversation.

The benefit of connectors: less hunting, copying, and switching between windows.

Without a connector

You find the document, copy the relevant text, and paste it into the chat. This is often enough for a one-off question.

With a connector

You ask the AI to find relevant information in a connected service. Some connections can also make changes. Check each connector’s supported actions. Read vs. write access ↗

Connect your first app in Claude
  1. Open Claude’s Customize → Connectors area and browse the directory.
  2. Pick a service you already use. Read its capabilities and permissions.
  3. Choose Connect or Install, then sign in to that service.
  4. Start with a small lookup: ask it to find one non-sensitive document and link the result.

Availability depends on the connector and account. Official setup instructions ↗

Give it the access the task needs.

Read access retrieves information. Write access can change things. Review permissions before connecting; choose narrower or read-only access where offered. Keep confirmation controls enabled for sending, deleting, or purchasing. A sentence saying “ask me first” is useful, but it does not replace permission settings.

Use trusted connectors and skills. Only share information you’re comfortable giving that service; follow school or workplace rules for their data. Review both the AI app’s and connected service’s privacy settings. Google connector permissions · Trusted skills

A starting point

“Plan a trip.”

The AI has to guess almost everything.

A more useful request

“Plan a two-day family trip to Chicago. We have a toddler, won’t have a car, and want to spend under $200 on activities. Give me a morning/afternoon schedule. Ask about anything important that’s missing.”

You can keep adjusting: “Less walking.” “Make it shorter.” “Explain that word.” Specific requests and follow-up questions are part of the conversation. Claude’s beginner tips ↗

Your turn: make the request clearer.

“Help me study.” What two details would you add? Think of your answer before opening the example.

See one possible answer

“Help me study beginner Spanish for 15 minutes. Quiz me on ordering food, one question at a time, and explain my mistakes.” The topic and time limit give context; the quiz instructions describe the format. Your wording can be different.

What if the first answer is bad?

Point to the part that missed: “These activities are too far apart. Keep each day in one neighborhood.” For current facts, ask it to search and link the original sources if web search is available. Open those links yourself. Asking “Are you sure?” is not an independent check.

Prompts to try.

Pick a prompt, copy it, and paste it into your chosen AI app. Edit the details to make it yours.

Learn something new

A patient study partner

Teach me how Wi-Fi works. I’m a complete beginner. Use an everyday analogy, explain one idea at a time, then ask me one question to check my understanding. Wait for my answer.

Make dinner easier

Work with what you have

Help me plan three simple vegetarian dinners for two people. I have rice, chickpeas, and tomatoes, and 30 minutes to cook each night. Give me a short shopping list. Ask about allergies before suggesting recipes.

Find the right words

Get a draft you can edit

Help me write a friendly message declining an invitation. Keep it warm, under 60 words, and don’t invent an excuse. Give me two versions: one casual and one a little more formal.

Try a connected app

Requires a file connector

In my connected files, look for a document called “Trip checklist.” If you find several, show me their titles and dates first. Summarize the selected checklist and link the original. Don’t change any files.

Pause and explain it back.

Try these without scrolling up. No score—just a chance to see what stuck.

1. You want AI to use the same dinner-planning method every week. What would help?

Reveal the explanation

A skill can package that reusable method. A connector would provide access to an app, but wouldn’t by itself describe your planning method. For a one-off request, written instructions in the chat are enough.

2. An AI organizer asks another AI worker to research venues. Which is the subagent?

Reveal the explanation

The worker researching venues is the subagent. The lead agent coordinates the bigger task and brings the results together.

3. You connect a calendar to find a free hour. Does that mean you should let it send invitations automatically?

Reveal the explanation

No. Finding availability and sending invitations are different actions. Check the connector’s permissions and confirmation settings. Review guests, dates, and time zones before approving a send.

Make it a five-minute experiment.

  1. Ask. Try one prompt in an AI app.
  2. Adjust. Change one thing: length, tone, budget, or level of detail.
  3. Check. Verify an important detail yourself.

You’re learning the useful habit: ask, refine, check.

The questions everyone asks.

No question is too basic.

Is AI the same as Google search?

Search helps you find pages. Generative AI creates a response. Some AI apps can also search the web and use those results. A polished answer alone does not tell you whether a live search happened; inspect the sources and the original pages. Checking AI responses

Can it see my email and everything on my phone?

It needs a supported connection or another explicit way to access that information, such as a file you upload or a screen you share. Don’t assume a claim like “I checked your inbox” is proof: look for the actual tool result and verify it in the original app. Claims about tool access

Can an agent still get things wrong?

Yes. Agents can make mistakes while planning, using tools, or interpreting results. If the answer matters, check it against an independent, reliable source. For health, legal, or financial decisions, involve an appropriate qualified professional. AI can help you prepare questions; it should not be your only authority. Accuracy limitations

Do I need to pay or learn to code?

You can begin by typing in an app; you don’t need programming skills. Try the access you already have before subscribing. Paid plans may add limits or capabilities, but a specific tool or connector can have its own requirements. Check the feature you want before paying.
