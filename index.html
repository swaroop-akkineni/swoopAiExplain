Choose an AI concept

An agent works toward a goal.

It can choose steps, use available tools, look at the results, and decide what to do next. A fixed automation follows preset steps; an agent can adapt its approach. Products use the term differently. Agent definition ↗

“Find two possible itineraries, compare them with our budget, and bring me a recommendation.”

In an app with suitable tools, it could search for activities, compare travel times, and revise the schedule. You review the result before booking.

Remember: the agent is the one doing the task.

MAKE IT HAPPEN.

A skill is a reusable playbook.

Think of it as a set of instructions your agent can use whenever they’re helpful. A skill can tell it what to include, what to check, and how you’d like the result to look. Skills explained ↗

A “family itinerary” skill could say: include nap breaks, group nearby stops, and finish with a packing list.

You could type those instructions each time. A skill saves you from explaining the same things over and over.

Remember: a skill gives your agent instructions it can use again.

KNOW THE HOW.

A connector links your agent to tools it can use.

With your permission, your agent can use connected tools to find information and get things done. What it can do depends on the tools you connect and the permissions you give it. Connector guide ↗

A calendar connection can let your agent check your calendar, schedule events, set reminders, and add a Zoom link to an event, depending on what the connected tools support.

Remember: a connector gives your agent access to tools.

GET CONNECTED.

A subagent takes part of the work.

Think of a manager delegating specific pieces of work to experts. A lead agent does something similar: it gives a focused task to another agent, called a subagent, then brings the results together. One subagent might focus on flights, another on hotels. In some systems, these subagents can work at the same time. How delegation works ↗

Like a manager planning a family trip, the lead agent asks one subagent to research museums and another to research outdoor activities, then brings their recommendations together.

This depends on the product. Typing “use three subagents” into an ordinary chat does not guarantee that separate agents run.

Remember: a subagent handles a piece of the task. When would a helper be useful?

TEAM UP.

Context is the information the AI has in front of it.

Think of briefing someone before they help you. Your request, relevant conversation details, instructions, and information brought in from files or tools can all be part of the context. Context explained ↗

“Plan a trip” gives little to go on. “Two adults, Lisbon in October, US$700 each for flights, and home by October 17” gives the agent details to work with.

Useful details help it tailor the answer. It can only take in a limited amount at once, so keep important requirements clear. Don’t assume it has every past conversation or knows a detail you haven’t shared.

Remember: context is what the agent has to work with right now.

Agent The big-picture thinker.
It asks questions, remembers the constraints in this conversation, chooses what to search, and updates the recommendation when you change your mind.
Skill The handy playbook.
A reusable “compare flights” playbook could say: check dates and airports, include baggage and fees, compare stops and travel time, and explain the tradeoffs.
Connector The link to real flight data.
An API is a way for software to request information from another service. A connector lets the agent use it. For example, Soar offers a flight-search MCP connection. MCP is a shared standard for connecting AI tools. The travel service supplies search results; the agent compares them.
Subagents Specific tasks for subagents.
One subagent could search flexible dates. After the results arrive, another could check baggage and fare conditions against your preferences. They report back to the lead agent, which combines the findings. Each subagent needs access to the tools and details its job requires.
Context The details that guide the answer.
Your dates, budget, baggage needs, and the flight results available to the agent help it compare options. “We need to be home by October 17” adds a requirement that changes which flights fit.

LET’S TALK ABOUT THE HELPERS

When and why
use subagents?

Use a helper when there’s a clear piece of work worth handing over. One lead agent still keeps track of the whole job and checks how the pieces fit.

Think of organizing a family trip. You could do it all yourself, or ask someone to investigate hotels and report back while you work on the rest. A subagent is that helper. You keep talking to the lead agent. Angie Jones explains delegation ↗

Three times a helper can be useful.

  • There are separate things to research. Once the dates are fixed, one helper can investigate hotels while another looks into activities. Working at the same time can save waiting.
  • One part involves lots of detail. A helper can read hotel reviews and return a shortlist with reasons and links. The lead gets the useful findings without carrying every research note through the rest of the task.
  • You want a separate check. After a plan is drafted, a helper can look specifically for missed requirements, such as an activity scheduled before the family arrives. It can catch mistakes, but it can also miss them.

Helpers can work together or one after another. Builder explains focused work and separate checks.

Could one agent still do it all?

Often, yes. Comparing two flight options or rewriting an invitation usually needs just one agent. Handing work over adds instructions, waiting, and checking, and can cost more. Use a helper for a clear benefit—not just because a task has several steps.

If all you need is the same checklist followed each time, that’s where a skill can help. You don’t need another worker just to reuse instructions.

Start with the result you want.

Before choosing helpers, describe what a good finished answer would look like. For this new example:

“Find a hotel and two activities for our family’s three-night Lisbon stay. We’ll reach the hotel at 10 p.m. Keep the hotel and activities within US$650 altogether. Bring us a plan to review.”

The lead first confirms the dates, who’s traveling, and any access needs. Then it can give two helpers the same trip details and different assignments.

See how the lead brings it together

Hotel A closes check-in an hour before the family arrives. The lead rules it out unless late arrival can be confirmed.

Hotel B plus the activities costs US$480 + US$140 = US$620, leaving US$30. The lead also checks that the activity times and travel from the hotel fit. If they do, it has a plan to recommend.

If something clashes, it asks for a specific change: “Find an activity we can reach from Hotel B by 10 a.m.” It checks the revised answer before presenting the plan. Simply pasting the two reports together would miss this work.
