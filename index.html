EXPLAINED

Choose an AI concept

An agent works toward a goal.

It can choose steps, use available tools, look at the results, and decide what to do next. A fixed automation follows preset steps; an agent can adapt its approach. Products use the term differently. Agent definition ↗

“Find two possible itineraries, compare them with our budget, and bring me a recommendation.”

In an app with suitable tools, it could search for activities, compare travel times, and revise the schedule. You review the result before booking.

Remember: the agent is the one doing the task.

MAKE IT HAPPEN.

A skill is a reusable playbook.

Think of it as a set of instructions your agent can use whenever they’re helpful. A skill can tell it what to include, what to check, and how you’d like the result to look. Skills explained ↗

A “family itinerary” skill could say: include nap breaks, group nearby stops, and finish with a packing list.

You could type those instructions each time. A skill saves you from explaining the same things over and over.

Remember: a skill gives your agent instructions it can use again.

KNOW THE HOW.

A connector links your agent to tools it can use.

With your permission, your agent can use connected tools to find information and get things done. What it can do depends on the tools you connect and the permissions you give it. Connector guide ↗

A calendar connection can let your agent check your calendar, schedule events, set reminders, and add a Zoom link to an event, depending on what the connected tools support.

Remember: a connector gives your agent access to tools.

GET CONNECTED.

A subagent takes part of the work.

Think of a manager delegating specific pieces of work to experts. A lead agent does something similar: it gives a focused task to another agent, called a subagent, then brings the results together. One subagent might focus on flights, another on hotels. In some systems, these subagents can work at the same time. How delegation works ↗

Like a manager planning a family trip, the lead agent asks one subagent to research museums and another to research outdoor activities, then brings their recommendations together.

This depends on the product. Typing “use three subagents” into an ordinary chat does not guarantee that separate agents run.

Remember: a subagent handles a piece of the task.

YOU

“Help me find a good flight deal from New York to Lisbon.”

TRAVEL AGENT

“Two adults, October 10–17, 2026. Up to US$700 each, round trip. JFK or Newark, dates flexible by two days. Economy, nonstop if possible, with one checked bag each.”

TRAVEL AGENT

“I’ll compare those dates and nearby dates, and check the total with bags. I can ask one subagent to explore date combinations and another to compare the returned fares.”

“Among the results returned, the best match is a US$680 nonstop round trip per person, October 11–18, with a checked bag. A US$620 option also includes a checked bag, but has a long stop. Both fit your budget; the nonstop saves travel time.”

YOU

“Actually, we need to be home by October 17.”

TRAVEL AGENT

“I’ll narrow the return dates and search again. Before any purchase, you’ll review the itinerary, current total, and fare rules, then explicitly approve.”

A whole team.

Agent The big-picture thinker.
It asks questions, remembers the constraints in this conversation, chooses what to search, and updates the recommendation when you change your mind.
Skill The handy playbook.
A reusable “compare flights” playbook could say: check dates and airports, include baggage and fees, compare stops and travel time, and explain the tradeoffs.
Connector The link to real flight data.
An API is a way for software to request information from another service. A connector lets the agent use it. For example, Soar offers a flight-search MCP connection. MCP is a shared standard for connecting AI tools. The travel service supplies search results; the agent compares them.
Subagents Specific tasks for subagents.
One subagent could search flexible dates. After the results arrive, another could check baggage and fare conditions against your preferences. They report back to the lead agent, which combines the findings. Each subagent needs access to the tools and details its job requires.

You keep talking to the lead agent. It coordinates the tools and subagents.

“Best” means best for your priorities among the offers searched. A cheaper ticket can cost more with bags or a long stop. Prices and availability can change, so a real agent must recheck the selected offer before purchase. Searching is separate from permission to book. How flight search works ↗

LET’S TALK ABOUT THE HELPERS

Could one agent
do it all?

Often, yes! An agent can work through many steps on its own. Helpers are optional: use them when dividing the work is useful.

For two flight options, one agent can compare the prices, bags, and arrival times. Now imagine researching a whole family trip: places to stay, activities, and everyone’s needs. There may be enough separate research to share out.

Helpers can investigate different things at the same time, each keeping its own working notes and reporting back. But explaining the assignments and checking their answers takes effort too. More helpers do not automatically mean a better answer. How this works in practice ↗

Same kind of agent. Different responsibility.

The lead agent keeps track of the whole request. A subagent is another agent given a piece of it. “Sub” describes who it reports to, not how clever it is. Calling it a specialist does not guarantee that its answers are right.

Start with the result you want.

Before choosing helpers, describe what a good finished answer would look like. For this new example:

“Find a hotel and two activities for our family’s three-night Lisbon stay. We’ll reach the hotel at 10 p.m. Keep the hotel and activities within US$650 altogether. Bring us a plan to review.”

The lead first confirms the dates, who’s traveling, and any access needs. Then it can give two helpers the same trip details and different assignments.

The assignments, prices, and results below are made up. The US$650 covers only the hotel and activities.

Pause here: would you choose the cheaper hotel?

See how the lead brings it together

Hotel A closes check-in an hour before the family arrives. The lead rules it out unless late arrival can be confirmed.

Hotel B plus the activities costs US$480 + US$140 = US$620, leaving US$30. The lead also checks that the activity times and travel from the hotel fit. If they do, it has a plan to recommend.

If something clashes, it asks for a specific change: “Find an activity we can reach from Hotel B by 10 a.m.” It checks the revised answer before presenting the plan. Simply pasting the two reports together would miss this work.

How do you decide what to hand over?

Try three questions:

  • Can I explain the piece clearly? “Find two hotels with these requirements” gives a helper a finish line.
  • Can it make progress separately? Hotel and activity research can start together once the dates and destination are fixed. The final schedule needs both results.
  • Will sharing it help enough? A broad search might benefit. Comparing two listings already in front of you probably does not.

There’s no magic number of steps that becomes “too much.” If one agent keeps missing requirements or takes too long, first clarify the task. Then consider a helper for a well-defined part. If the parts need constant back-and-forth, keep them together.

How do you actually set this up?

In an app that supports helpers, some setup may already be done for you. You describe the goal and constraints; the app may let its lead agent decide what to delegate. Other apps let someone define reusable helpers beforehand.

Whoever sets up a helper gives it a job description, the information it needs, the tools it may use, and what to return. For our hotel helper, that means the trip brief, search access, and a short list with prices and evidence. A request to “use helpers” cannot add a feature the app does not support.

Orchestration means coordinating that work: assigning tasks, waiting for results, passing on relevant details, asking for corrections, and checking the finished answer. The app can follow a preset order or let the lead choose the next step.

The lead must pass along relevant details; helpers may not see your whole conversation. If a helper cannot find or confirm something, it should report that gap. Searching for a hotel also does not give it permission to book.

Try it with something familiar.

For a family reunion, one helper researches venues and another researches catering. Both return good options. What would you check before combining them?

One thing the lead should catch

Does the venue allow outside catering? If it doesn’t, the two recommendations may not work together. The lead needs an allowed caterer or a different venue. If catering depends entirely on the chosen venue, choose the venue first.

Start with one agent. Give helpers clear pieces when useful. Check that the pieces work together.
